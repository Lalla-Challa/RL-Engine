# CAUSAL CONTINUITY MODEL

### A Qualitative Framework for Building Living, Causally Coherent Fictional Worlds

**Version 1.0**

---

# 1. Purpose

The **Causal Continuity Model (CCM)** is a framework for constructing fictional worlds in which events feel as though they emerge naturally from the world rather than appearing solely because the author needs them for the plot.

The purpose of CCM is not to eliminate plot structure, coincidence, dramatic design, randomness, or authorial control.

Its purpose is to make those things operate within a world that appears to have its own continuity.

A well-functioning CCM world should give the impression that:

* people have lives beyond the current scene,
* places continue to exist when nobody is looking at them,
* systems continue operating independently of the protagonist,
* characters make decisions using the information actually available to them,
* events leave consequences behind,
* unrelated circumstances can intersect,
* and important events arise from previously established conditions.

The central question of CCM is:

> **How can a fictional world feel as though it would continue to function even if the author stopped directly controlling each individual moment?**

---

# 2. Foundational Concept

CCM is based on a simple causal observation:

> **What appears to be an action may itself be the consequence of something earlier, while becoming the cause of something later.**

The distinction between "action" and "reaction" is therefore not fundamental.

An event may simultaneously be:

* the consequence of an earlier event,
* a change to the current world state,
* and a cause of later events.

The practical interpretation is:

> **Every meaningful consequence can become a cause.**

A fictional world therefore develops through continuing changes in state:

> **Existing State → Event → Changed State → New Possibilities**

The framework uses this idea as a model of fictional causality.

It does not claim that the real universe is deterministic, nor does it require every event in fiction to have one inevitable outcome.

---

# 3. Core World Structure

CCM represents a fictional world using five fundamental domains:

1. **Actors**
2. **Environments**
3. **Systems**
4. **Events**
5. **Uncertainty**

These domains interact continuously.

---

# 4. ACTORS

An **Actor** is any dynamic entity capable of participating in change.

Actors may include:

* humans
* animals
* robots
* artificial intelligences
* aliens
* supernatural beings
* organizations treated as collective entities

Narrative labels such as:

* protagonist
* antagonist
* hero
* villain
* side character
* supporting character
* NPC

are not fundamental CCM categories.

They are narrative roles assigned to Actors.

The same Actor can have different narrative importance at different points in a story.

---

# 5. ACTOR ATTRIBUTES AND STATE

Every important Actor should be understood through two different kinds of information.

## Attributes

Attributes are relatively persistent characteristics.

Examples include:

* age
* species
* occupation
* physical characteristics
* personality tendencies
* medical conditions
* socioeconomic circumstances

## State

State describes what is true about the Actor **at a particular moment**.

Examples include:

* current location
* physical condition
* emotional condition
* fatigue
* current goals
* current intentions
* current resources
* current beliefs
* current relationships
* immediate circumstances

### Example

**Attribute:**

> Kaden is eighteen years old.

**State:**

> Kaden is exhausted, experiencing chest pain, standing on a hill, and wants to return home.

The distinction is:

> **Attributes describe what an entity is. State describes what is currently true about that entity.**

State changes constantly. Attributes may remain unchanged.

---

# 6. ACTOR VARIABLES

The following variables may be useful when modeling an Actor.

## Persistent attributes

* age
* species
* occupation
* personality tendencies
* physical characteristics
* medical conditions
* socioeconomic circumstances

## Current state

* physical condition
* emotional condition
* location
* resources
* immediate circumstances
* intentions
* current relationships

## Internal variables

* goals
* desires
* fears
* beliefs
* assumptions
* knowledge
* expectations

## External variables

* environment
* relationships
* available resources
* restrictions
* obligations
* threats
* opportunities

## Capabilities and constraints

These describe what the Actor:

* can do
* cannot do
* is willing to do
* is prevented from doing

CCM does not require these variables to be numerical. Qualitative descriptions are sufficient.

---

# 7. ACTOR AGENCY

Actors should act according to their current internal and external conditions.

Relevant factors may include:

* goals
* desires
* fears
* beliefs
* knowledge
* misconceptions
* relationships
* resources
* capabilities
* restrictions
* environment
* physical state
* emotional state

An Actor does not need to make the objectively smartest decision.

A decision only needs to be plausible for that Actor given:

* what they want,
* what they know,
* what they believe,
* what they can do,
* what they cannot do,
* and what circumstances surround them.

This permits:

* mistakes
* impulsive decisions
* irrational decisions
* emotional decisions
* self-destructive decisions
* misunderstandings

without requiring the characters to behave like puppets following the author's intended outcome.

---

# 8. ACTOR KNOWLEDGE

A fictional world contains information that is not equally available to every Actor.

For each important Actor, distinguish between:

### Known facts

Information the Actor actually knows.

### Beliefs

What the Actor thinks is true.

### Misconceptions

Beliefs that are false.

### Uncertainty

Things the Actor does not know or cannot determine.

### World truth

What is actually true, regardless of what any Actor believes.

The world state and an Actor's knowledge of that state are therefore not identical.

This distinction supports:

* secrets
* misunderstandings
* dramatic irony
* mistaken decisions
* mystery
* incomplete investigations

---

# 9. RELATIONSHIPS

Relationships should be treated as state-bearing objects rather than simple labels.

A relationship may include:

* familiarity
* trust
* affection
* resentment
* dependence
* fear
* obligation
* respect
* expectations
* shared history
* knowledge about one another

These properties can change independently.

For example, two Actors may have:

* high familiarity
* low trust
* moderate affection
* high resentment

A new Event may change only one or two of those variables.

The purpose is not to create a rigid numerical relationship system, but to recognize that relationships themselves have state.

---

# 10. ENVIRONMENTS

An **Environment** is the physical, social, or conceptual context in which Actors and Events exist.

Examples include:

* homes
* hospitals
* streets
* cities
* schools
* forests
* battlefields
* countries
* spacecraft
* online communities

An Environment is not merely scenery.

It has its own state and can therefore influence Events.

For example, a hospital may be:

* empty
* overcrowded
* understaffed
* operating normally
* under emergency conditions

The same location can therefore produce different possibilities under different states.

---

# 11. ENVIRONMENT VARIABLES

Relevant Environment variables may include:

* location
* physical condition
* social condition
* population
* available resources
* accessibility
* ongoing activity
* environmental conditions
* restrictions
* infrastructure
* current occupants

An Environment should continue functioning even when the protagonist is absent.

A hospital does not freeze because a character leaves.

A city does not stop operating because a chapter changes location.

---

# 12. SYSTEMS

A **System** is a mechanism that governs, constrains, enables, or transforms possible states.

The original "Nature" category is expanded into Systems because many different mechanisms perform the same fundamental function.

Examples include:

## Natural systems

* physics
* biology
* weather
* disease
* aging
* ecosystems

## Social systems

* law
* government
* economics
* customs
* institutions
* social hierarchy

## Technological systems

* transportation
* communication
* machinery
* computing
* infrastructure

## Fiction-specific systems

* magic
* supernatural rules
* psychic abilities
* fictional physics
* alien biology
* time manipulation

A magic system belongs in the same structural category as gravity because both determine what the world permits and how states can change.

---

# 13. SYSTEM RULES

A System may determine:

* what can happen
* what cannot happen
* what conditions are required
* what effects follow
* how quickly something may occur
* what constraints exist
* what exceptions are allowed

Systems should operate independently of the protagonist unless the fictional world explicitly makes them protagonist-dependent.

---

# 14. WORLD STATE

The **World State** is the complete set of currently relevant conditions.

At a given moment, the world may contain:

* Actor states
* Environment states
* System conditions
* relationships
* unresolved consequences
* pending processes
* available uncertainties
* active constraints

An Event changes part of this state.

The changed state then becomes the starting condition for later Events.

Therefore:

> **An Event does not merely happen. It changes what can happen afterward.**

---

# 15. EVENTS

An **Event** is the smallest **causally meaningful state transition**.

The key word is **meaningful**.

Not every physical occurrence needs to be represented separately.

A character:

* blinks
* breathes
* moves a finger
* shifts their weight

These are occurrences, but they normally do not deserve individual Event status.

An Event should be represented separately when its state change has meaningful consequences for what can happen afterward.

Examples include:

* making a decision
* learning a secret
* getting injured
* meeting someone
* losing trust
* discovering evidence
* changing location in a consequential way
* experiencing a major medical change
* causing an environmental change
* dying

---

# 16. THE CAUSAL SIGNIFICANCE TEST

To determine whether an occurrence deserves Event-level representation, ask:

> **If this occurrence were removed or substantially changed, would the plausible future state of the world meaningfully change?**

If the answer is no, the occurrence may be compressed into another Event or Episode.

If the answer is yes, it deserves greater causal attention.

This is the primary method for determining Event scale.

The question is not:

> "Did something happen?"

The question is:

> **"Did something happen that matters to what can happen afterward?"**

---

# 17. EVENT SCALE

CCM uses multiple levels of resolution.

## Micro-event

A very small state transition.

Examples:

* chest tightness begins
* a phone vibrates
* a character notices something
* an object falls

Micro-events only need to be represented separately when their details matter.

## Event

A causally meaningful state transition.

Examples:

* Kaden stops running
* a secret is revealed
* trust is broken
* a character learns critical information
* two people meet

## Episode

A connected group of Events forming one recognizable activity.

Example:

> Kaden runs the hill, develops symptoms, stops, recovers, and returns home.

## Scene

A larger dramatic unit containing one or more Episodes.

## Chapter

A broader narrative unit containing one or more Scenes and/or major Episodes.

---

# 18. CAUSAL COMPRESSION RULE

Consecutive occurrences should be combined into a larger unit when separating them does not reveal a meaningful causal difference.

They should be separated when an internal occurrence changes the state enough to alter future possibilities.

For example:

> They talk → joke → walk → laugh

may be represented as one Episode.

But:

> They talk → secret revealed → trust changes → argument → reconciliation

should normally be represented through multiple Events because the internal transitions matter.

Similarly, a very small action such as a hug may deserve its own Event if it substantially changes the relationship.

Therefore:

> **Event scale is determined by causal significance, not physical size, duration, or spectacle.**

---

# 19. EVENT STRUCTURE

A meaningful Event may be examined through four properties.

## Trigger

What existing conditions allowed or produced the Event?

## Transition

What changed?

## Persistence

How long does the resulting change remain relevant?

## Propagation

What later possibilities are affected by the change?

These are analytical tools.

They do not need to be formally written out for every Event.

---

# 20. CAUSAL LOAD

Not all Events deserve the same level of causal scrutiny.

**Causal Load** measures how strongly later states, Events, or interpretations depend on an Event.

There are two primary classes.

## Load-bearing Event

An Event whose removal or substantial alteration would produce meaningful downstream divergence.

Its consequences support a significant part of the later narrative or world state.

## Connective Event

An Event whose removal or alteration would require only local rewriting while leaving the broader causal trajectory substantially intact.

Connective does not mean meaningless.

A connective Event may still provide:

* atmosphere
* pacing
* texture
* movement
* personality
* minor state changes
* continuity

The distinction concerns **causal burden**, not literary value.

---

# 21. DYNAMIC CAUSAL LOAD

Causal Load is not necessarily fixed when an Event occurs.

A seemingly minor Event may later become important.

Therefore:

> **An Event may acquire greater Causal Load when later Events become dependent upon it.**

This is called **Deferred Causal Load**.

Example:

> Kaden buys an ordinary lighter.

Initially:

> Connective Event.

Later:

> The lighter becomes critical evidence.

The earlier occurrence has now acquired significant downstream importance.

This allows apparently ordinary Events to become meaningful without requiring the author to treat every small detail as deliberate foreshadowing from the beginning.

---

# 22. CAUSAL SCRUTINY GATE

Before deeply analyzing an Event, determine its Causal Load.

## If the Event is Connective

Run the **Non-Contradiction Check**.

If it passes, continue.

A complete causal derivation is not required.

## If the Event is Load-bearing

Run the **Full Causal Test**.

This prevents the framework from requiring detailed justification for every ordinary action.

---

# 23. NON-CONTRADICTION CHECK

A Connective Event is provisionally valid if it does not contradict established information.

Check the Event against:

* Actor Attributes
* Actor State
* Actor Knowledge
* Environment conditions
* System rules
* prior Events
* established constraints

The key distinction is:

> **An unexplained Event is not automatically an invalid Event.**

For example:

> Two strangers are in the same café at 9 AM.

No complete derivation is required.

The Event becomes invalid only if existing information contradicts it.

For example:

* one person has been established to never leave home before noon,
* the café has been established as closed,
* one person is currently hospitalized elsewhere,
* a System explicitly prevents the event.

Therefore:

> **Absence of explanation is permitted. Contradiction is not.**

---

# 24. FULL CAUSAL TEST

Load-bearing Events receive deeper analysis.

Ask the following.

## Existing State

What is true immediately before the Event?

## Actors

Which Actors cause or participate in it?

## Motivation

Why would those Actors behave this way given their current:

* goals
* knowledge
* beliefs
* constraints
* relationships
* circumstances?

## Environment

Why is the Event possible in this Environment?

## Systems

What world mechanism permits, constrains, or produces it?

## Uncertainty

Could accident, coincidence, incomplete information, or uncontrolled external circumstances contribute?

## Consequence

What changes because the Event occurs?

## Propagation

What later states or Events become possible or impossible because of that change?

## Independence

Would the preceding conditions still make sense without the later narrative outcome?

The final question is primarily a test for Load-bearing Events.

---

# 25. CAUSAL INDEPENDENCE

**Causal Independence** asks whether an Event and the conditions leading to it possess a legitimate existence independent of the later narrative outcome.

This is primarily a test for Load-bearing Events.

Connective Events are normally exempt unless they:

* contradict established information, or
* later acquire significant causal load.

The objective is not to prove that every event had a single necessary cause.

The objective is to prevent important Events from existing solely because the later plot requires them.

---

# 26. NO RETROACTIVE MOTIVATION

Actors should act according to information available to them at the time.

Therefore:

> **A future Event cannot serve as the motivation for a past decision unless an established System allows access to future information.**

Exceptions may include:

* prophecy
* precognition
* time travel
* divine knowledge
* other explicitly established mechanisms

In such cases, the relevant System must provide a mechanism that makes future information available.

---

# 27. INDEPENDENT TRAJECTORIES

Actors, Environments, and Systems should possess trajectories that exist independently of the desired narrative outcome.

An important Actor should have reasons to exist beyond the plot.

Ask:

> Would this Actor still make this decision if the protagonist did not exist?

For Environments:

> Would this place still function if the protagonist never entered it?

For Systems:

> Would this System continue operating if the protagonist were absent?

The answer does not always have to be yes.

The purpose of the test is to prevent the entire world from behaving as though it was created solely to serve one character.

---

# 28. INTERSECTION OF TRAJECTORIES

Independent trajectories can intersect.

This is one of the primary ways natural encounters and coincidences emerge.

For example:

* one character has a medical appointment,
* another character has a medical appointment,
* a hospital has changing scheduling conditions,
* transportation affects arrival times,
* another patient's emergency causes a delay.

These independently established trajectories may intersect.

The result:

> **The two characters meet.**

The meeting does not require destiny.

It does not require an impossible coincidence.

It emerges from the interaction of existing trajectories.

---

# 29. COINCIDENCE

CCM does not prohibit coincidence.

Coincidence is a normal part of life.

The distinction is between:

## Legitimate coincidence

Independent conditions happen to intersect.

## Manufactured coincidence

The world introduces otherwise unsupported circumstances solely to force a desired narrative outcome.

Therefore:

> **Coincidence is permitted. Contradiction is not.**

An unexplained coincidence is not automatically invalid.

If it is compatible with established reality and does not carry enough Causal Load to require deeper investigation, it may simply happen.

---

# 30. WORLD AUTONOMY

A living fictional world continues operating outside the immediate narrative focus.

People continue doing things.

Relationships change.

Weather changes.

Institutions operate.

Animals behave.

Technology fails.

Markets move.

Politics develop.

Illness progresses.

Places change.

The protagonist does not pause reality by leaving the room.

The author does not need to describe all of these processes.

They only need to remain possible within the established world.

---

# 31. INCOMPLETE INFORMATION

The complete causal history of the world does not need to be visible to the reader.

Actors also possess incomplete information.

Therefore:

> **An Event may have an underlying causal basis without its complete cause being known or explained.**

This is necessary because reality contains enormous numbers of events whose exact causes observers never discover.

CCM requires causal compatibility, not universal exposition.

---

# 32. THREE LEVELS OF CAUSAL KNOWLEDGE

A cause may exist at one of three narrative knowledge levels.

## Known Cause

The Actor or reader knows why the Event occurred.

## Plausible Cause

The exact cause is not explicitly known, but the Event is compatible with the established world.

## Unknown Cause

The cause is genuinely unresolved.

All three are permitted.

A story does not need to explain every Event merely because the author has considered causality.

---

# 33. IMPOSSIBLE VS. UNEXPLAINED

These are different conditions.

## Unexplained

The cause has not been provided.

## Impossible

The Event contradicts the established world.

CCM permits unexplained Events.

CCM rejects impossible Events unless an established System change makes them possible.

This distinction is especially important for Connective Events.

---

# 34. AUTHORIAL CONTROL

CCM does not attempt to remove the author.

Instead, it changes how authorial control is exercised.

The author establishes:

* Actors
* Environments
* Systems
* initial conditions
* constraints
* relationships
* important variables
* narrative goals
* narrative constraints

The author may then select among causally legitimate possibilities.

The guiding rule is:

> **The author controls the conditions; the conditions constrain the possibilities; the author selects among those possibilities.**

The author should not arbitrarily override established conditions merely to force a desired outcome.

---

# 35. WORLD-BEFORE-PLOT TEST

For major Events, ask:

> **Would this world still behave this way if the intended plot outcome did not exist?**

If yes, the world likely possesses causal autonomy.

If the only justification is:

> "It happens because I need the characters to meet."

the Event requires stronger grounding.

---

# 36. REMOVAL TEST

To determine Causal Load, ask:

> **What changes if this Event is removed?**

If removal requires only local rewriting while the broader trajectory remains substantially intact:

> **Connective.**

If removal causes major downstream divergence:

> **Load-bearing.**

The Removal Test determines how deeply the Event should be investigated.

---

# 37. CAUSAL NETWORK

A fictional world should not be treated as one simple linear chain.

Multiple causes can converge on one Event.

One Event can create multiple consequences.

Therefore, causal structure should be treated as a **network**.

Example:

> Kaden's condition → doctor appointment

> Girl's condition → family accompanies her

> Hospital circumstances → waiting-room changes

> Transportation circumstances → timing changes

These trajectories may intersect:

> **Kaden sees the girl.**

This structure allows complexity without requiring destiny or authorial forcing.

---

# 38. UNCERTAINTY

A living world contains things that no Actor completely controls or predicts.

These may include:

* accidents
* timing variations
* forgetfulness
* mechanical failures
* environmental changes
* unexpected encounters
* incomplete information
* unknown System interactions

Uncertainty should be treated as part of the world.

It is not a license to introduce arbitrary Events whenever convenient.

---

# 39. THE DRAFTING MODE

CCM should not function only as a debugging system.

It can also generate candidate Events.

The drafting process begins with the **current World State**, not with a blank page.

The generative loop is:

> **Current State → Active Pressures → Candidate Events → Selection → Event → Consequences → New State**

---

# 40. ACTIVE PRESSURES

A **Causal Pressure** is an unresolved force currently pushing some part of the world toward change.

Pressures may come from:

* Actor goals
* Actor desires
* Actor fears
* habits
* constraints
* medical conditions
* relationship tension
* resource shortages
* deadlines
* Environment changes
* System behavior
* unresolved consequences
* external disturbances
* narrative constraints

Pressures are not Events.

They are conditions from which Events may emerge.

---

# 41. EVENT GENERATION

Candidate Events may be generated from several sources.

## Actor-generated

An Actor chooses, attempts, avoids, reveals, hides, or changes something.

## State-generated

A current condition produces a transition.

Example:

> illness worsens.

## Environment-generated

An environmental condition changes.

Example:

> a road becomes inaccessible.

## System-generated

A System produces a consequence.

Example:

> weather changes.

## Interaction-generated

Independent trajectories collide.

Example:

> two characters unexpectedly encounter each other.

The generator should produce **possibilities**, not automatically declare a single outcome.

---

# 42. POSSIBILITY FIELD

A given World State can support multiple legitimate future Events.

The system may therefore produce a **candidate set**.

For example:

Current state:

* Kaden is home
* it is early morning
* he has a running habit
* his heart condition is known
* nobody is supervising him

Possible Events might include:

* he runs,
* he skips the run,
* he receives a phone call,
* he prepares breakfast,
* his symptoms change,
* he leaves for the hospital.

The role of the generator is to create the possibility field.

The role of selection is to choose among those possibilities.

---

# 43. NARRATIVE CONSTRAINTS

A **Narrative Constraint** is a desired condition that the story should eventually reach.

It is not an immediate Event and must not be treated as a causal explanation.

Examples:

> Kaden and the girl eventually meet.

> Kaden eventually receives her heart.

> The protagonists never verbally say "I love you."

> Kaden remains unmarried.

Narrative Constraints guide exploration without overriding Actor agency or causality.

---

# 44. TYPES OF NARRATIVE CONSTRAINTS

## Hard Constraint

Must eventually be satisfied.

Example:

> Kaden receives the girl's heart.

## Soft Constraint

Strong preference but not absolutely required.

Example:

> Their first meeting is quiet.

## Forbidden Constraint

A condition that must not occur.

Example:

> They never explicitly confess their love.

## Temporal Constraint

A condition tied to time or sequence.

Example:

> They meet before the transplant.

## Range Constraint

A variable must remain within a defined conceptual boundary.

Example:

> Kaden remains emotionally guarded throughout the story.

---

# 45. NARRATIVE SELECTION

The world can generate many legitimate possibilities.

The author or a higher-level planner selects among them using considerations such as:

* causal legitimacy
* character relevance
* narrative usefulness
* thematic relevance
* pacing
* novelty
* unresolved pressures
* narrative constraints

The key distinction is:

> **Causality generates the menu. Narrative judgment chooses the meal.**

The system should not confuse "possible" with "worth narrating."

---

# 46. CAUSAL LOAD VS. NARRATIVE WEIGHT

These are separate concepts.

## Causal Load

How much downstream state depends on the Event.

## Narrative Weight

How valuable the Event is for telling the story.

An Event can have:

* high Causal Load and high Narrative Weight,
* low Causal Load and high Narrative Weight,
* high Causal Load and low Narrative Weight,
* or low values for both.

Example:

> Kaden sees the girl for the first time.

It may initially have moderate Causal Load but high Narrative Weight.

An ordinary hospital scheduling change may have moderate Causal Load but low Narrative Weight.

The two measures should not be collapsed into one.

---

# 47. GENERATIVE LOOP

CCM's drafting loop is:

## 1. Establish Current State

Determine what is currently true.

## 2. Identify Active Pressures

Determine what is pushing Actors, Environments, and Systems toward change.

## 3. Generate Candidate Events

Produce legitimate possibilities from those pressures and conditions.

## 4. Validate

Reject Events that contradict established reality or violate Systems.

## 5. Evaluate

Consider Causal Load, Narrative Weight, and Narrative Constraints.

## 6. Select

Choose a candidate Event.

## 7. Apply Consequences

Determine what changes.

## 8. Update State

The resulting conditions become the next World State.

## 9. Repeat

Generate again from the new state.

---

# 48. SIMULATION AND STORY GENERATION

A symbolic CCM engine can operate without a language model.

Its core job can be:

> **Generate a world history from structured Actors, Environments, Systems, Rules, States, and Uncertainty.**

The system does not need to generate polished prose.

It can generate:

* Events
* event logs
* causal relationships
* state changes
* Episodes
* scene skeletons

A separate language layer can later turn those structures into prose.

---

# 49. STORY EXTRACTION

A simulated world may generate many low-value Events.

Not all of them should appear in the final narrative.

A **Story Extractor** identifies Events and sequences that deserve narrative attention based on:

* Causal Load
* Narrative Weight
* character relevance
* relationship changes
* thematic relevance
* unresolved consequences
* narrative constraints
* surprise
* escalation
* continuity

The simulation can therefore contain more Events than the final story shows.

---

# 50. EPISODE CLUSTERING

Related Events can be grouped according to:

* participants
* location
* time
* causal relationship
* thematic relationship
* shared activity

For example:

> running → symptoms → stopping → recovery → returning home

may become:

> **Episode: Kaden's morning hill run**

rather than five disconnected narrative objects.

---

# 51. SCENE CONSTRUCTION

The Scene Builder converts selected Events into a scene skeleton.

A scene skeleton may contain:

* scene purpose
* location
* time
* participants
* entry state
* active pressures
* important Events
* turning point
* exit state
* unresolved threads
* important information
* continuity notes

The skeleton does not need to contain finished prose.

---

# 52. EXAMPLE SCENE SKELETON

## Scene: Hospital Waiting Room

**Purpose**

Introduce the heroine and establish the contrast between her life and Kaden's.

**Location**

Hospital waiting room.

**Time**

Morning.

**Participants**

Kaden, the girl, her parents, medical staff.

**Entry State**

Kaden has completed his examination and wants to leave.

The girl has arrived with her parents.

**Active Pressures**

Kaden:

* wants independence
* dislikes being controlled

Girl:

* wants autonomy
* is frustrated by being treated as fragile

Parents:

* want to protect her

Hospital:

* operating under current scheduling conditions

**Events**

1. Kaden waits.
2. The girl arrives with her parents.
3. Her parents fuss over her.
4. She objects.
5. Kaden notices the interaction.
6. The girl notices Kaden.
7. They briefly make eye contact.
8. Kaden leaves.

**Exit State**

Kaden and the girl now know of one another's existence.

Their relationship has not yet become established.

---

# 53. DEBUGGING MODE

CCM can be used after Events have already been written.

The diagnostic loop is:

> **Event → Causal Load → Validation → Causal Analysis → State Update**

Its purpose is to locate:

* contradictions
* forced motivations
* impossible behavior
* unsupported major Events
* broken consequences
* continuity errors
* world dependence on plot convenience

---

# 54. DRAFTING MODE VS. DEBUGGING MODE

CCM has two primary uses.

## Drafting Mode

> **State → Pressure → Possibilities → Selection → Event → State**

Used to generate content.

## Debugging Mode

> **Event → Load → Validation → Analysis → State**

Used to inspect content.

The same world model supports both modes.

---

# 55. WORLD AUTONOMY TEST

A world feels increasingly autonomous when:

* Actors possess independent goals,
* Environments continue changing independently,
* Systems continue operating independently,
* consequences persist,
* trajectories intersect without requiring destiny,
* characters lack information they should not possess,
* and the author does not need to invent special circumstances for every major transition.

The objective is not complete simulation.

The objective is **causal credibility**.

---

# 56. THE CENTRAL EVENT FILTER

Every suspicious Event should pass through four levels.

## Gate 1 — Causal Load

Is the Event:

* Connective?
* Load-bearing?

## Gate 2 — Non-Contradiction

Does it conflict with established:

* Attributes?
* States?
* Knowledge?
* Environments?
* Systems?
* Events?
* constraints?

## Gate 3 — Causal Rigor

Only for Load-bearing Events:

* What conditions produced it?
* Who participated?
* What did they know?
* Why did they act?
* What Systems support it?
* What Environment supports it?
* Could uncertainty contribute?
* What changed?
* What consequences follow?
* Is the setup independent of the later payoff?

## Gate 4 — State Update

What is different after the Event?

# 57. THE MOST IMPORTANT LIMITATION

CCM does not require:

> **Everything to be derived in exhaustive detail.**

It requires:

> **Important causal transitions to be grounded.**

Ordinary connective transitions only need to remain compatible with what has already been established.

This creates two protections.

### Too little causality

> "They meet because the story needs them to meet."

This feels manufactured.

### Too much causality

> "Why did Character A choose the café at 9:03 rather than 9:04?"

This makes fiction unnecessarily difficult to write.

Causal Load determines how much scrutiny an Event receives.

---

# 58. THE CORE PRINCIPLES

CCM can be summarized through the following statements:

> **The world has a state.**

> **Events emerge from that state.**

> **Events change the state.**

> **Changed states create new possibilities.**

> **Every consequence can become a cause.**

> **Important Events require causal grounding.**

> **Ordinary Events only need to avoid contradiction.**

> **Actors act from what they know, want, can do, and are constrained by.**

> **Actors possess trajectories independent of the plot.**

> **Environments possess trajectories independent of the plot.**

> **Systems continue operating independently of the protagonist.**

> **Independent trajectories may intersect.**

> **Coincidence is permitted. Contradiction is not.**

> **An unexplained Event is not automatically invalid.**

> **A contradictory Event is invalid unless the world contains a mechanism that permits it.**

> **A small Event may acquire large Causal Load later.**

> **The author establishes conditions but should not arbitrarily override them.**

---

# 59. MASTER LAW

## Master Law of Causal Continuity

> **Every meaningful Event must emerge from the existing state and governing Systems of the world; meaningful Events alter that state, and their consequences become conditions for subsequent Events. The level of causal scrutiny should be proportional to the Event's Causal Load.**

The practical version is:

> **State creates possibilities. The author selects. Events change state. Consequences create new possibilities.**

The philosophical version is:

> **Every consequence can become a cause.**

# 60. PRACTICAL CHECKLIST

When developing a story, first establish:

### World

* What Actors exist?
* What Environments exist?
* What Systems govern the world?
* What uncertainties are possible?

### Actors

* What are their Attributes?
* What is their current State?
* What do they want?
* What do they know?
* What do they believe?
* What can they do?
* What can't they do?
* What relationships do they have?

### Environments

* What is happening here?
* What resources exist?
* What restrictions exist?
* What is changing?

### Systems

* What mechanisms are operating?
* What do they permit?
* What do they prevent?
* What conditions do they require?

### Current State

* What is true right now?
* What pressures are active?
* What unresolved consequences exist?

### Generation

* What Events could emerge from the current state?
* Which Events are supported by Actor behavior?
* Which Events come from Environment or Systems?
* Where might independent trajectories intersect?

### Selection

* Which candidate Event is most useful?
* Is it consistent with the world?
* What narrative constraint does it serve?
* What will it change?

### Causal Load

* Is it Connective?
* Is it Load-bearing?
* Could its importance increase later?

### Validation

* Does it contradict anything established?
* If Load-bearing, does it have a plausible causal basis?
* Does it create consequences that the world can support?

### State Update

* What is now different?
* What new possibilities exist?
* What old possibilities have disappeared?

Then continue from the new state.

## Final conceptual model

The complete CCM architecture can therefore be understood as:

> **WORLD → STATE → PRESSURE → POSSIBILITY → SELECTION → EVENT → CONSEQUENCE → STATE**

with three governing laws:

> **State Continuity**
> **Consequence Propagation**
> **Independent Trajectories**

and one governing constraint on effort:

> **Causal scrutiny is proportional to Causal Load.**

The model is intended to make fiction **causally alive**, not mechanically over-explained. It should allow the ordinary, unexplained, accidental, and coincidental parts of life to exist freely while demanding greater rigor from the Events that carry the story.
